Care folder checklist for an elderly parent in the UK

Organise contacts, appointments, current medicines information, documents and everyday routines. It works on paper and does not require Lilica or any other app.

Last reviewed: 3 November 2026Review owner: Luxford Interactive Ltd
All resources
Important: This is a general organisation aid, not medical, legal or financial advice. It does not replace a care plan, medicines list, professional assessment or instructions from a health or care professional. Lilica is not an emergency service. For a life-threatening emergency call 999.[1] If someone needs medical help now and it is not life-threatening, use the appropriate urgent-care service for your UK nation; in England, Scotland and Wales this may include NHS 111. Northern Ireland has different urgent-care arrangements.[2]

Supporting a parent can involve dozens of small details held in different places. A care folder gives you one index of what matters and where to find it. It can be a ring binder, a secure digital folder, or a mixture of both.

You do not need to complete everything today. Start with contacts, current medicines information and the next appointment. Involve your parent and follow their wishes wherever possible. Caring for someone does not automatically give another person permission to see every record, manage money or make decisions for them.

Before you begin

  • Ask your parent what they want recorded.
  • Agree who may see each section.
  • Choose where the folder will be kept.
  • Decide who will update it and how often.
  • Keep originals of legal and identity documents securely; use the folder as an index unless a copy is genuinely needed.
  • Do not put passwords, PINs, full card details, authentication codes, safe combinations or key-safe codes in a shared folder.

Printable care folder checklist

1. Quick contacts

Keep enough information to reach the right person. Do not copy information that is not needed.

  • Parent's preferred name and contact details
  • Home address and postcode
  • Main family/friend contact
  • Other agreed helpers and what each person does
  • GP surgery
  • Usual pharmacy
  • Relevant hospital clinic or care team
  • Council adult social care contact, if involved
  • Paid care provider, if involved
  • Emergency contact named by your parent
  • Communication needs, preferred language or interpreter requirement

Notes / changes:

2. Current health and medicines information

Use information from current labels, prescriptions, care plans or health professionals. Do not guess or change instructions.

  • Current medicines list: name, strength, form and directions exactly as supplied
  • Known allergies or adverse reactions, as recorded by a clinician
  • Where medicines are normally kept
  • Who orders repeat prescriptions and the usual method
  • GP, pharmacy and relevant clinical contacts
  • Current care plan or clinical instructions — or a note saying where the official copy is held
  • Equipment supplied and provider contact details
  • Date the list was last checked against current labels/prescription

The NHS says repeat prescriptions in England can be ordered through the NHS App or website, other online services, or the GP surgery; arrangements vary, so record the method your parent's surgery actually uses.[3]

Do not use this folder to decide whether to start, stop, change or share a medicine. Ask a pharmacist, prescriber or relevant health professional if anything is unclear.

3. Appointments and care contacts

  • Next appointments, including date, time and location
  • Transport or accessibility arrangements
  • Questions your parent wants to ask
  • Who is attending, if anyone
  • Clinic letters or a note of where they are stored
  • Follow-up actions, owner and due date
  • Contact route for changing an appointment

Next appointment: ____________________________________________ Questions / actions:

4. Everyday routine and preferences

This is not a clinical assessment. Record only what your parent wants a helper to know.

  • Preferred name and form of address
  • Typical daily routine
  • Food and drink preferences
  • Mobility or communication support already agreed
  • Religious, cultural or personal preferences
  • Important activities, pets and social contacts
  • What usually helps a day go smoothly
  • Things your parent does not want others to do

Most important preferences:

5. Practical household information

  • Regular shopping, deliveries and household tasks
  • Utility/provider contact routes if relevant
  • Equipment servicing contacts
  • Home access arrangement shared only with authorised people
  • Pet care arrangements
  • Transport arrangements
  • Where essential supplies are kept

Do not place security codes or detailed access instructions in a broadly shared copy. Record who holds that information instead.

6. Money and administration

A carer does not automatically have authority to use another person's account or act for them.

  • Bills or renewals that your parent wants help tracking
  • Benefits or council contacts, if relevant
  • Name of an authorised attorney/appointee, if one exists
  • Location of official paperwork
  • Professional adviser contact, if your parent wants this included
  • Review dates for recurring payments or services

Do not copy full bank/card details, PINs, passwords or identity documents into the shared folder. Seek official or legal guidance if authority to manage affairs is unclear.

7. Assessments and formal support

In England, an adult unpaid carer can ask their local council for a free carer's assessment. It is separate from the needs assessment for the person receiving care, although the two can be requested together.[4] Social care systems and terminology differ across the UK, so check the route where your parent lives.

  • Needs assessment requested/completed, if relevant
  • Carer's assessment requested/completed, if relevant
  • Council care and support plan — or location of official copy
  • Personal budget/direct payment information, if applicable
  • Paid care plan and provider contacts, if applicable
  • Review date and questions to raise

NHS guidance says a care and support plan may record what is important to the person, what they can do, equipment or care needed, how and when support happens, and who to contact with questions.[5] Your folder should point to the current formal plan, not replace it.

8. If the usual carer cannot help

  • Agreed first person to contact
  • Backup contact(s)
  • Essential tasks for the first 24 hours
  • Where the latest medicines information and formal plans are held
  • How authorised backup helpers get the information they need
  • Council/provider contingency contact, if one exists
  • Date the arrangement was last confirmed

Create a separate, limited-access contingency plan rather than giving everyone access to the whole folder.

10. Review page

Review after a change in medicines, contacts, address, support arrangements or formal plans. Otherwise choose a realistic regular check.

  • Remove information that is no longer needed.
  • Replace outdated lists; mark the newest version clearly.
  • Confirm that named helpers still agree to the role.
  • Check who can see each section.
  • Test phone numbers and links.
  • Confirm where the folder and official documents are kept.

A simple 20-minute start

If the full checklist feels too much, do only these five things:

  1. Write down the GP, pharmacy and two agreed contacts.
  2. Add or locate the current medicines list.
  3. Record the next appointment.
  4. Note where the formal care plan and important documents are held.
  5. Agree who can access the folder and when it will be checked again.
