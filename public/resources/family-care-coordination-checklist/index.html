Family care coordination checklist

Agree roles, updates, task ownership and clearer handovers. It works on paper and does not require Lilica or any other app.

Last reviewed: 3 November 2026Review owner: Luxford Interactive Ltd
All resources
Important: This is a general organisation aid, not medical, legal or financial advice. It does not replace a care plan, medicines list, professional assessment or instructions from a health or care professional. Lilica is not an emergency service. For a life-threatening emergency call 999.[1] If someone needs medical help now and it is not life-threatening, use the appropriate urgent-care service for your UK nation; in England, Scotland and Wales this may include NHS 111. Northern Ireland has different urgent-care arrangements.[2]

When several people are helping, good intentions can still lead to crossed wires. This checklist helps a family or group of trusted people agree who is doing what, what needs to be shared, and how to hand over without making one person carry everything.

“Family” here can include friends, neighbours or anyone chosen by the person receiving support. Start with that person's wishes. Share the minimum information each helper needs for their agreed role.

Start with a calm conversation

You do not need a perfect system or a large meeting. A 20-minute conversation with the person you support and the people already helping is enough to begin.

Person receiving support: _____________________________________ What they want help with: ____________________________________ What they prefer to do themselves: ____________________________ People they agree can be involved: ____________________________ Date agreed: __________________ Next review: _______________

  • Ask what help is wanted — do not assume.
  • Agree what is private and what may be shared.
  • Include the person in decisions and updates in the way they prefer.
  • Check each helper's availability and limits.
  • Identify gaps without pressuring one person to fill all of them.
  • Record the official contacts or plans that this checklist points to.

NHS guidance says an English council care and support plan can include what is important to the person, what they can do, what help is needed, how and when support will happen, and who to contact with questions.[3] A family checklist can sit alongside a formal plan, but should not overwrite or replace it.

Printable coordination checklist

1. Agree one shared goal

Choose a practical goal for the next week or month.

Our current goal is:

We will know it is organised when:

Examples: transport is arranged for the next appointment; shopping days are covered; everyone knows who to contact about the paid care visit.

2. List recurring tasks

Tick only what is relevant. This is an organisation list, not a care assessment.

  • Calls or social visits
  • Shopping or meal support
  • Transport
  • Appointments and questions
  • Collecting prescriptions, as authorised
  • Household tasks
  • Bills or paperwork, where there is proper authority
  • Contact with council, NHS or care provider
  • Paid care coordination
  • Pet care
  • Breaks and cover for the main carer
  • Other: ______________________________________________________

3. Give each task an owner and a backup

Rules that reduce confusion:

  • The owner has agreed, not merely been assigned.
  • “Done” is clear enough that someone else can recognise it.
  • The due date is realistic.
  • A backup knows they are the backup.
  • Medical decisions remain with the person and relevant professionals.
  • Financial actions are only taken by someone with appropriate authority.

4. Choose one place for each kind of information

Avoid copying the same detail into many chats and documents.

Do not use a family group chat for passwords, PINs, full payment-card details, authentication codes, key-safe codes or unnecessary copies of health and identity documents. A chat participant can forward or screenshot information, and old participants may retain it. Agree a more restricted location for sensitive details.

ICO guidance says organisations using personal data should explain why they use it, what types they use, how long it is kept and who receives it.[4] A family group is not the same as an organisation, but those questions are a useful privacy check: why do we need this, who needs it, and when can it be removed?

5. Set an update rhythm

Choose the lightest method that works.

  • Urgent practical change: contact ______________________________
  • Routine update channel: _____________________________________
  • Weekly/monthly check-in: ____________________________________
  • Task completion format: _____________________________________
  • Who closes old threads/tasks: ________________________________
  • When inactive helpers lose access: ___________________________

A useful update is short and factual:

What happened: _____________________________________________ What is next: ______________________________________________ Owner and due date: ________________________________________ Does the person need to decide anything? ____________________

Do not diagnose, interpret symptoms or change professional instructions in an update. If medical advice is needed, use the appropriate health service.

6. Prepare for appointments

  • Confirm the person wants someone else involved.
  • Write down their questions in their own words.
  • Bring or locate the current medicines list if relevant.
  • Confirm transport and accessibility arrangements.
  • Agree who will take notes.
  • Record only the follow-up actions the person agrees to share.
  • Put each action in the task list with an owner and date.

Next appointment: ____________________________________________ Person attending: ____________________________________________ Questions:

7. Make handovers clear

Use this when another person takes over a visit or task.

Handover date/time: __________________________________________ From / to: ___________________________________________________

  • What has already been done?
  • What still needs doing, by when?
  • Has anything changed in the formal plan or provider schedule?
  • Where is the current medicines information? (Do not copy unless needed.)
  • Which preferences matter for this visit/task?
  • Who should be contacted with a practical question?
  • What information must not be shared further?

Short handover note:

8. Notice overload and gaps

This is not a test of commitment. People's work, health, distance, money and caring responsibilities differ.

  • Is one person holding most tasks or knowledge?
  • Are any important tasks unowned?
  • Does the main carer have a realistic backup?
  • Does anyone need a smaller role or a pause?
  • Would a local carers service, council assessment or paid support help?
  • Does the person receiving support still agree with the arrangement?

In England, an adult unpaid carer can ask their local council for a free carer's assessment. It considers how caring affects their life and may identify support; it is separate from the needs assessment for the person receiving care.[5] Arrangements differ across the four nations, so use Carers UK's nation selector or local carers service to find the relevant route.[6]

9. Agree what happens when plans change

  • If an owner cannot do a task, they tell: ______________________
  • Backup tries: _______________________________________________
  • If no helper is available, contact: __________________________
  • If the paid service changes, contact: ________________________
  • If medical help is needed now, use the appropriate NHS route.
  • If there is a life-threatening emergency, call 999.

Do not use the family task list or Lilica to wait for an emergency response.

10. Ten-minute review

  • Ask the person receiving support whether the arrangement still suits them.
  • Close completed tasks.
  • Reassign tasks only with agreement.
  • Remove outdated information and access.
  • Check the next appointment and any deadlines.
  • Confirm backup cover.
  • Set the next review date.

Reviewed by: __________________________ Date: _______________ One change we agreed: ________________________________________ Next review: __________________________________________________

When people disagree

Pause the logistics and return to three questions:

  1. What does the person receiving support want?
  2. What decision actually needs to be made now?
  3. Is this a family preference, a professional instruction or a legal question?

Record the point of disagreement without blaming anyone. Ask the relevant professional, advocate or official service when the question is beyond the family's role. Do not use this checklist to decide that someone lacks capacity or that another relative has authority.
