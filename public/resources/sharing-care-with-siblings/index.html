How to share caring for a parent with your siblings (UK)

When one sibling lives nearest, it is easy for them to end up doing almost everything. These steps help a family share the load more evenly.

Last reviewed: 5 November 2026Review owner: Luxford Interactive Ltd

Looking after a parent is rarely planned. One person starts helping, then helps a little more, and a year later they are the default for everything. The others may not realise how much is happening, or may not know how to help.

This guide is for brothers, sisters and other relatives who want to share the work more fairly. Start with what your parent wants. They stay at the centre of every decision, and information about them should only be shared as they are comfortable with.

1. Make the work visible

Most disagreements start because the work is hidden. Sit down and write a plain list of everything that happens in a typical month: appointments, lifts, shopping, phone calls with the GP surgery, paperwork, prescriptions collected, bills checked, home visits.

  • Ask the person doing most of it to list what they do, without apology.
  • Add the invisible jobs: remembering dates, chasing replies, worrying, being the first call when something goes wrong.
  • Note which tasks need someone to be local and which can be done from anywhere.

2. Split tasks by what each person can really do

Fair does not have to mean identical. A sibling who lives far away may not be able to do school-run style lifts, but could take over phone calls to the council, ordering repeat prescriptions online (where your parent has agreed), or booking appointments.

  • Give each task one named owner and one backup.
  • Agree how often each person will check in, and keep the time realistic.
  • Include paid help or money towards it where that is the better contribution.
  • Plan breaks. The main carer needs regular time off, not just when they are exhausted.

3. Agree how you will keep each other informed

Updates scattered across several group chats get lost. Pick one place for each kind of information and tell everyone where it is.

  • One shared calendar for appointments and visits.
  • One list for open tasks, with who is doing what.
  • A short summary after each appointment, written the same day.
  • A weekly or fortnightly ten-minute call so nobody is surprised.

4. Talk about the hard parts early

Some topics are easier to raise before a crisis: who will step in if the main carer is ill, what happens if your parent needs more help, and who can speak to professionals on their behalf. Decisions about health or money stay with your parent where they can make them. Arrangements such as a power of attorney have to be set up properly, so look at the official guidance rather than assuming a relative automatically has authority.[3]

5. When you disagree

Disagreement is normal, and it is often about feeling unseen rather than the task itself. Try to:

  • Start from "what does Mum or Dad want?" rather than "what is fair to me?"
  • Describe facts, not character: "I did the last five hospital visits" is easier to hear than "you never help".
  • Agree one small change, then review it in a month.
  • Consider a neutral person, such as a carers organisation, if talks keep stalling.[1]

6. Look after the person doing the most

Unpaid carers may be able to get support, including a carer's assessment from the local council in England.[2] Passing that information to the sibling doing most of the caring is a practical way to help.
