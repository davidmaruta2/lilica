How to keep track of an elderly parent's appointments (UK)

Between the GP, hospital clinics, dentist and optician, it is easy to lose track. A few simple habits make appointments less stressful for everyone.

Last reviewed: 5 November 2026Review owner: Luxford Interactive Ltd

When you help a parent with appointments, the hard part is often not the visit itself. It is remembering what was agreed last time, who is taking them, what to bring, and what happens next. This guide shows a simple routine you can use on paper or in any app.

Important: This is general organising advice, not medical advice. Follow the instructions you are given by health professionals. In a life-threatening emergency call 999.[1] If someone needs medical help now and it is not life-threatening, use the urgent-care service for your UK nation; in England, Scotland and Wales this may include NHS 111.[2]

1. Keep every appointment in one place

Choose a single calendar and put everything in it. Paper wall calendars, a shared phone calendar and an organising app all work. What matters is that it is the only place you look.

  • Enter each appointment as soon as the letter, text or phone call arrives.
  • Include the place, the time to leave, and who is going.
  • Add a reminder the day before and a reminder to arrange transport.
  • Put the contact number of the clinic or surgery in the entry in case you need to change it.

2. Prepare before you go

Appointments are short. Writing things down beforehand helps you and your parent use the time well.

  • Write down the top three things you want to ask or mention.
  • Note any changes since the last visit, in your parent's words where you can.
  • Bring the current medicines list or the medicines themselves, if your parent agrees.
  • Bring any letters, test results or notes from the last appointment.
  • Check access needs: glasses, hearing aids, a walking aid, an interpreter or a quiet space.

3. Take a short note at the appointment

With your parent's agreement, jot down what was said. You do not need to write everything.

  • What was decided or changed?
  • What are we supposed to do next, and by when?
  • Who do we contact if something changes or we are worried?
  • Ask the professional to repeat or explain anything that was unclear.

4. Write up the outcome the same day

Memory fades quickly. Before the day is over, copy your notes into one record and share the outcome with the others who help, if your parent is happy for you to.

  • Date, who you saw, and the main points.
  • Any follow-up appointments or tests, added to the calendar straight away.
  • Any task for someone else, with a name beside it.

5. Keep the paperwork findable

Keep letters and results together in one folder, in date order, with the newest at the front. Our care folder checklist lists what to include.

6. Share the load

If several people help, agree who usually goes and who is the backup. Carers UK has a guide to arranging care and support that covers sharing information and getting help.[3]
