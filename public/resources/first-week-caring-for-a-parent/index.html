Caring for a parent for the first time: what to do in the first week (UK)

Suddenly caring for someone can feel overwhelming. You do not need to solve everything at once. Start with these steps.

Last reviewed: 5 November 2026Review owner: Luxford Interactive Ltd

If you have just started helping a parent or relative, you may be dealing with new appointments, worry and a lot of unfamiliar words. This guide gives you a short, sensible order to work in. Most of it can be done in a few days, and none of it needs to be perfect.

Important: This is general information, not medical, legal or financial advice. In a life-threatening emergency call 999.[1] If someone needs medical help now and it is not life-threatening, use the urgent-care service for your UK nation; in England, Scotland and Wales this may include NHS 111.[2] Rules differ across England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

Day 1 to 2: get the basics down on one page

  • Their full name, date of birth, address and NHS number if you have it.
  • GP surgery, pharmacy and any hospital clinics, with phone numbers.
  • Who is already helping, including neighbours, relatives and paid carers.
  • A list of current medicines, copied from the labels or an official list, not from memory.
  • Two emergency contacts, and who has a spare key.

Day 2 to 3: talk with them about what they want

Ask what help they would like and what they want to keep doing themselves. Ask who they are happy for you to talk to, such as the GP or the council. Write down what they say. This keeps their wishes at the centre, and it helps if anyone else has to step in later.

Day 3 to 4: line up some support for you

Caring takes time and energy, and support exists for carers as well as for the person being cared for.

  • Read the NHS overview of support and benefits for carers.[3]
  • In England, you can ask your local council for a carer's assessment, which looks at the impact of caring on your life. Other UK nations have their own routes.[4]
  • Check whether you might be eligible for Carer's Allowance. Rules about hours of care and earnings apply, so check the official page.[5]

Day 5: think about authority and paperwork

A relative does not automatically have the right to make decisions about another adult's health or money. A lasting power of attorney is one way a person can choose someone to act for them, but it has to be set up while they have the capacity to do so, and the rules differ across the UK.[6] You do not need to decide this now, but it is worth knowing the options. Take advice if you are unsure.

Day 6 to 7: plan for a bad day

  • Who could step in if you were ill? Write down names and numbers.
  • Where is the medicines list and the key information kept, and does a backup person know?
  • When could you take a proper break this month?

Our carer contingency plan organiser is a printable way to do this.

What can wait

You do not have to sort out every detail in week one. Longer-term questions, such as paid care, housing and finances, can be tackled step by step once you know what is needed. Asking for help early is not a failure; it is part of caring well.
